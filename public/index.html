+I've had several conversations this week with VC and PE partners, and value...
The Bridge		Jun 19193——06
Full post content
I've had several conversations this week with VC and PE partners, and value creation kept coming up. My view that GTM and sales acceleration are the primary active return levers received mixed reactions: in PE, accepted reality, but in VC, still slightly heretical. The PE logic is clean: you buy the company, the clock starts, and over a 3-4 year hold, you have a finite set of levers you can actually pull. You're not re-architecting the product. You're not waiting for the market to mature. What you can move in the time you have is the commercial engine: pricing, motion, win rates, revenue per rep. Operating partners like me live there because that's where the return is. VC reflexes run the other way: product and market first, find fit, and the rest follows. GTM gets treated as downstream of PMF, a VP Sales you bring in eighteen months later. Fine at pre-see, of course, but it is expensive at (scaling up) seed and Series A, when growth stalls and the board reaches for the only two explanations the reflex allows: the product needs more, or we need more reps. Neither is right, in my experience. Go through the last twenty closed-lost deals, and what you find is almost always architecture, or the lack of it. The motion that closed the first ten customers ran on the founder being in the room, and, of course, nobody wrote it down. So every rep now sells it differently, and the pipeline reflects optimism rather than evidence. The product was rarely the problem. The motion was never built. This is where the comparison breaks down, and PE fixes it by taking control and embedding operators, while VC can't (a minority stake, at most a board seat, a founder who has to want the help, etc.). Same lever, pulled differently. For any serious VC, that's the case for a platform function and for GTM operating partners inside it. Not a VP Sales parachuted into one company, but a commercial capability the fund can point to across the whole portfolio, that diagnoses the motion before the board defaults to "hire more reps." I resisted this conclusion for a long time, but PE worked this out a decade ago. VC is where PE was before operating partners became table stakes. The commercial engine is the lever and the only question is whether you've built the function to pull it. View on LinkedIn →

Pardon my morbid humor, but there's a reason for this dynamic. The OP owns the value creation plan but doesn't run the company. Full accountability for the numbers, no formal authority to deliver them. It puts them in a difficult position. When their influence doesn't work and the CEO won't move, pressure becomes the only lever available to them. And the pressure is real. Existential even. Hiring/firing the management team is increasingly in the OP's scope of control (56%. wield this authority). The OP is there to "help" you, but the OP is also there to replace you if need be. And for good reason. It's deeply personal for them. Today, ~80% of OPs are carry-eligible, with $3M to $8M riding on the companies they oversee. I don't know about you, but if I had 7-figures riding on the success of someone else, I'd turn into one of those overzealous parents at a little league game. So part of this pressure is just them protecting their own money. But if both CEO and OP have the same financial incentives, why is there so much tension? It boils down to underlying psychology. Perceived threats to autonomy, identity, and legacy. You can't spreadsheet these challenges. Genius in financial engineering and deal sourcing doesn't help here. There is no silver bullet solution, but a few things I've seen help CEOs and OPs work together: (1) Assess and establish chemistry pre-transaction (or as early as possible), rather than forcing an OP on your CEO out of left field. (2) Define what the OP can actually decide, so authority stops being a guessing game. (3) Get a neutral read on the relationship, because an OP can't be objective about a relationship they're standing in the middle of. Pressure makes diamonds. But it also makes hostages. A mis-timed senior GTM hire is not a single line in one company's P&L because if you multiply that misread across the portfolio, it stops being an operating cost and starts dragging DPI. The first time I sat in this pattern I was inside one of the companies, and I read it as our problem and our bad hire. It took a second and a third company, from a different seat, to realise I had been misreading a structural pattern as a local accident. Funding a VP of Sales in each company buys you competent execution and a single data point. Funding the seat that watches them all buys pattern recognition early enough to change the outcome. The second is a returns lever, and a returns lever has to be senior, inside the investment decision, and paid like the partners it sits next to. And, like it wasn't enough, the bar moved again in the past 18 months or so: AI stopped being a theme and became operating infrastructure: inside portfolio companies, in go-to-market, in code, in how deals get researched. +I've had several conversations this week with VC and PE partners, and value...

Full post content
I've had several conversations this week with VC and PE partners, and value creation kept coming up. My view that GTM and sales acceleration are the primary active return levers received mixed reactions: in PE, accepted reality, but in VC, still slightly heretical. The PE logic is clean: you buy the company, the clock starts, and over a 3-4 year hold, you have a finite set of levers you can actually pull. You're not re-architecting the product. You're not waiting for the market to mature. What you can move in the time you have is the commercial engine: pricing, motion, win rates, revenue per rep. Operating partners like me live there because that's where the return is. VC reflexes run the other way: product and market first, find fit, and the rest follows. GTM gets treated as downstream of PMF, a VP Sales you bring in eighteen months later. Fine at pre-see, of course, but it is expensive at (scaling up) seed and Series A, when growth stalls and the board reaches for the only two explanations the reflex allows: the product needs more, or we need more reps. Neither is right, in my experience. Go through the last twenty closed-lost deals, and what you find is almost always architecture, or the lack of it. The motion that closed the first ten customers ran on the founder being in the room, and, of course, nobody wrote it down. So every rep now sells it differently, and the pipeline reflects optimism rather than evidence. The product was rarely the problem. The motion was never built. This is where the comparison breaks down, and PE fixes it by taking control and embedding operators, while VC can't (a minority stake, at most a board seat, a founder who has to want the help, etc.). Same lever, pulled differently. For any serious VC, that's the case for a platform function and for GTM operating partners inside it. Not a VP Sales parachuted into one company, but a commercial capability the fund can point to across the whole portfolio, that diagnoses the motion before the board defaults to "hire more reps." I resisted this conclusion for a long time, but PE worked this out a decade ago. VC is where PE was before operating partners became table stakes. The commercial engine is the lever and the only question is whether you've built the function to pull it.

I've seen this play out so often in the past 9 years in my roles. The tension is quiet at seed, bites at Series A-B, and can take over everything beyond that. Agreed the OP owns the value creation plan, but the plan moves through the relationship with the CEO and the leadership team, or it doesn't move at all. In my experience, when pressure becomes "the only lever," that's the tell that the relationship was never really built. Easier said than done, but it's worth building it. Maxwell Salazar Ask any private equity-backed CEO how they feel about their Operating Partner. Then watch them pick every word carefully, like a hostage in a proof-of-life video. Pardon my morbid humor, but there's a reason for this dynamic. The OP owns the value creation plan but doesn't run the company. Full accountability for the numbers, no formal authority to deliver them. It puts them in a difficult position. When their influence doesn't work and the CEO won't move, pressure becomes the only lever available to them. And the pressure is real. Existential even. Hiring/firing the management team is increasingly in the OP's scope of control (56%. wield this authority). The OP is there to "help" you, but the OP is also there to replace you if need be. And for good reason. It's deeply personal for them. Today, ~80% of OPs are carry-eligible, with $3M to $8M riding on the companies they oversee. I don't know about you, but if I had 7-figures riding on the success of someone else, I'd turn into one of those overzealous parents at a little league game. So part of this pressure is just them protecting their own money. But if both CEO and OP have the same financial incentives, why is there so much tension? It boils down to underlying psychology. Perceived threats to autonomy, identity, and legacy. You can't spreadsheet these challenges. Genius in financial engineering and deal sourcing doesn't help here. There is no silver bullet solution, but a few things I've seen help CEOs and OPs work together: (1) Assess and establish chemistry pre-transaction (or as early as possible), rather than forcing an OP on your CEO out of left field. (2) Define what the OP can actually decide, so authority stops being a guessing game. (3) Get a neutral read on the relationship, because an OP can't be objective about a relationship they're standing in the middle of. Pressure makes diamonds. But it also makes hostages. The governance structure you create decides which one you get.

The first time I sat in this pattern I was inside one of the companies, and I read it as our problem and our bad hire. It took a second and a third company, from a different seat, to realise I had been misreading a structural pattern as a local accident. Funding a VP of Sales in each company buys you competent execution and a single data point. Funding the seat that watches them all buys pattern recognition early enough to change the outcome. I have not sat in on a VC fund review this year where operational value creation wasn't named as the lever, but naming it and building to pull it are two different things. The deal math made the shift for everyone: a 2015 buyout, half funded on cheap debt, riding a rising multiple, needed about 5% annual EBITDA growth to clear 2.5x over a five-year hold. For deals underwritten between 2010 and 2022, cheap debt and multiple expansion did roughly 59% of the work, so that tailwind is priced out. So the return has to come from inside the company now. Margin, pricing power, add-on integration, compounding revenue, the works. "Our ICP is defined." The head of sales at a portfolio company (Series A, $2.9m revenue) told me this recently with complete confidence, but what they'd actually defined was two job titles joined by an "or": VP of Engineering or Head of Platform. That's a persona, and starting with the persona is the right instinct, because they carry the job-to-be-done and feel the pain you are leaning into. But a persona on its own is not a target. The same title is a different buyer depending on the company behind it. For example, a Head of Engineering at Facebook and a Head of Engineering at a 40-person startup share almost nothing: different budget, authority, pain, reason to move. Same job, opposite drivers. The persona tells you who feels the pain, but the ICP tells you which companies make that pain funded, urgent and repeatable. Skip that second half, and you hit one of two walls: --> Happy ears: you get the meeting, the nod, the "this really matters", and then nothing moves. No driver behind the pain, no budget, no mandate. You read enthusiasm as pipeline, and it dies in "no decision." --> Or silence: you can't get the meeting at all, because your positioning speaks to a driver this persona doesn't carry here. Low replies, dead outbound, "not a priority." Both look exactly like a product problem, and many decide they haven't found product-market fit and go back to build more, or pivot away. Look, often the pain is real, the persona is right, and the account is wrong. That is not a PMF gap to me, but an ICP and positioning gap that looks identical from the inside.
