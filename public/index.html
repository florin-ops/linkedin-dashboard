I have spent years as the operator a fund that calls when a portfolio company's commercial engine stalls, which makes the next part awkward, because I have come to think the model I am part of is quietly broken. In S&P Global's 2026 survey, 71% of GPs now say they prioritise operational value creation over financial engineering. The returns that used to come from multiple expansion and cheap leverage (roughly 59% of buyout returns for over a decade, per McKinsey) are gone, and everyone in the room knows it. What private equity has not fully settled is the staffing. It bought the operations thesis and found it had not hired enough people who can deliver it, so it named the gap: the operator shortage. VCs have the same shortage, but they just haven't named it yet. I am sure you know the patterns: a company stalls, the board finds a great operator, that operator fixes the one company and leaves with everything they learned. Six months later, another company stalls in the same way, and the fund starts from scratch. This type of help does not compound for the fund. I've written the full argument: why venture's version of the operator shortage gets solved at the fund level, not hiring help for portfolio companies one at a time, and why the minority-stake objection doesn't hold. Value Creation		Jul 6431——08
Full post content
One of the portfolio companies I am working with ($6.3M revenue, SaaS) closed a funding round about 5 months ago, aiming to break into new market segments by hiring 10 new sales reps. 5 months in, they've only found 4 great ones. What would you do? Would you hire the other 6 mediocre ones? This is no longer just a hiring issue, as there is also a shift around profitability that I am seeing more often these days. Margin expansion used to be a PE conversation, as opposed to VCs wanting growth, any growth, ARR on slide 3 and not much else after that. Today I don't sit in a single VC portfolio review meeting where unit economics doesn't come up in the first ten minutes. AI compressed the time it takes to get a product to market (sometimes to an MVP in weeks), and the conversation moved from the top line to what's underneath it. Back to my portfolio company: the founder wants 10 reps on the headcount slide, and the old instincts kick in, whoever's driving it: 6 mediocre hires get you closer to 10 than admitting you only found 4. But I believe that instinct is a time bomb. Mediocre reps don't grow revenue: they increase the cost base, and quietly erode profitability. And CAC has nowhere left to hide anymore. The founder wants a number. What they actually need is a sales leader or the operating partner willing to say "4 is the right number, here's the plan to get to 10 without diluting quality." Yes, I know that's a harder conversation, but it's also the one that helps you survive the next fundraise. Later edit: and no, I am no advocating for hiring AI Sales reps. That does not change the profitability equation, in spite of what many preach these days. View on LinkedIn →

+I spent most of my operating years inside one company at a time, and from...
The Platform Case		Jul 20287——14
Full post content
I spent most of my operating years inside one company at a time, and from that seat you only ever see one experiment running. An operator inside a single company sees one motion and one hiring cycle play out, while an operator sitting across a portfolio sees the same failure forming in three companies before any of the three founders has felt it. That is the asset a single VP of Sales can never be. I have learned that the failures repeat because they are structural, not local. And whether you are on the investor side or inside the company, we have all seen this sequence run. For example, a founder-to-team transition in which the first senior sales hire underperforms, and the founder blames the hire. Or hiring a VP of Sales at roughly £150K fully loaded, allowing 9 months to deliver results, underperformance, then unwinding the role. Looks like a misshire, but in reality, it is structural. Each was attempting to scale sales by adding senior talent to a go-to-market motion that had never been clearly defined or proven. Headcount went up, but so did burn, and progress often stalled. That is where the fund maths hurts. A mis-timed senior GTM hire is not a single line in one company's P&L because if you multiply that misread across the portfolio, it stops being an operating cost and starts dragging DPI. Capital going into headcount before the motion exists easily turns into five-year holds carrying the interest on that mistake. And I will confess I did not see this from the outside. The first time I sat in this pattern I was inside one of the companies, and I read it as our problem and our bad hire. It took a second and a third company, from a different seat, to realise I had been misreading a structural pattern as a local accident. Funding a VP of Sales in each company buys you competent execution and a single data point. Funding the seat that watches them all buys pattern recognition early enough to change the outcome. One is a cost line. The other is underwriting. View on LinkedIn →

+I have not sat in on a VC fund review this year where operational value...
The Platform Case		Jul 9268——03
Full post content
I have not sat in on a VC fund review this year where operational value creation wasn't named as the lever, but naming it and building to pull it are two different things. The deal math made the shift for everyone: a 2015 buyout, half funded on cheap debt, riding a rising multiple, needed about 5% annual EBITDA growth to clear 2.5x over a five-year hold. Today, with more expensive debt and a multiple that has stopped climbing, the same return requires closer to 10-12% earnings growth per year. For deals underwritten between 2010 and 2022, cheap debt and multiple expansion did roughly 59% of the work, so that tailwind is priced out. So the return has to come from inside the company now. Margin, pricing power, add-on integration, compounding revenue, the works. When I got into the industry about 9 years ago, operational value creation was just a line in the deck, but the deal math turned it into the job. Ok, nowadays most funds agree it is a lever, but still resource it like overhead. There is the operating bench that reviews the deck and joins the quarterly board call. And there is the operator who sits in the room when the deal is underwritten, owns a number, and carries it to exit. The first is a cost line. The second is a returns lever, and a returns lever has to be senior, inside the investment decision, and paid like the partners it sits next to. And, like it wasn't enough, the bar moved again in the past 18 months or so: AI stopped being a theme and became operating infrastructure: inside portfolio companies, in go-to-market, in code, in how deals get researched. A fund treating it as a slide is already behind the one building the capability in-house. PE built its version of this answer a decade ago: operating partners with real authority over the asset. VC and growth funds hold minority stakes, so control is exactly what they don't have. Copying the PE model wholesale doesn't work. The mechanism has to be different, and based on my platform experience, I have written up what that looks like. If your fund is weighing where operating capability should sit and what it should be paid, message me, and I will send it over. View on LinkedIn →

The Platform Case		Jul 27199——01
Full post content
In 10 years of portfolio engagements, I have not yet seen the original set of assumptions survive contact with the evidence. Every stalled company arrives with one already written. More reps. A new commercial leader. A pricing change. The prescription seems to be written months before anyone runs a diagnosis, and the capital behind it gets deployed against a framing nobody has tested. Usually the founder's own framing, which is the framing that produced the stall. What I usually do instead is unremarkable on paper: diagnose, review, act. What is different is how rarely it runs in that order, and what happens to portfolio outcomes when it does. Run it once, and you have fixed one company. Run it the same way across the book and commercial health becomes something a fund can actually read, before the board deck says so. One company's diagnosis is an engagement. The same arc across ten companies is an instrument. +"Our ICP is defined." The head of sales at a portfolio company (Series A...
The Method		Jul 23133——00
Full post content
"Our ICP is defined." The head of sales at a portfolio company (Series A, $2.9m revenue) told me this recently with complete confidence, but what they'd actually defined was two job titles joined by an "or": VP of Engineering or Head of Platform. That's a persona, and starting with the persona is the right instinct, because they carry the job-to-be-done and feel the pain you are leaning into. But a persona on its own is not a target. The same title is a different buyer depending on the company behind it. For example, a Head of Engineering at Facebook and a Head of Engineering at a 40-person startup share almost nothing: different budget, authority, pain, reason to move. Same job, opposite drivers. The persona tells you who feels the pain, but the ICP tells you which companies make that pain funded, urgent and repeatable. Skip that second half, and you hit one of two walls: --> Happy ears: you get the meeting, the nod, the "this really matters", and then nothing moves. No driver behind the pain, no budget, no mandate. You read enthusiasm as pipeline, and it dies in "no decision." --> Or silence: you can't get the meeting at all, because your positioning speaks to a driver this persona doesn't carry here. Low replies, dead outbound, "not a priority." Both look exactly like a product problem, and many decide they haven't found product-market fit and go back to build more, or pivot away. Look, often the pain is real, the persona is right, and the account is wrong. That is not a PMF gap to me, but an ICP and positioning gap that looks identical from the inside. Same product, put in front of the company with the driver, moves. So before you rebuild the product, check that you are not selling the right pain to the wrong company. Here's the cheat sheet I used with any early-stage portfolio company I work with. Hope it helps someone. 👇 From persona to ICP View on LinkedIn →
