In this market, I believe the winners will be the VC funds that have built go-to-market into the portfolio as infrastructure, rather than offering it as advice on the side. Why? Private credit has cooled on the sectors it used to love most: software. For the best part of a decade, enterprise software was the loan every lender wanted because it offered recurring revenue, low churn, and cash flows you could forecast years out. The exits VCs are waiting for, the acquisitions and the recaps, all get financed on the back of exactly that conviction. That conviction is fading now, and some of the numbers already show it, with US software buyouts running at roughly half of last year's pace and the deals that do clear pricing far wider than they did two years ago. I get it, because would you underwrite a 5-year hold when AI could rewrite the product within 24 months? >>> for PE, margin expansion stops being a cost story because you can only cut so far. The next leg of value has to come from the top line, which means getting serious about the go-to-market motion, opening new segments, and reworking the product itself. >>> for VCs, the hold gets longer. Portfolio companies wait longer to be bought, and that waiting room is brutal. They have to survive the go-to-market game, hold their ground against better-funded rivals, and build a sales motion that wins mid-market and enterprise accounts, capturing the early majority and not just the early adopters. And here's the difference: PE has an expert bench of operators who can step in and run the playbook, while VC doesn't. Yes, a VC is not a PE firm, so without a majority stake, there is less control and less influence. But that's exactly why the funds that treat go-to-market as infrastructure, not advice, are the ones I'd back to come out ahead. View on LinkedIn →

+I've had several conversations this week with VC and PE partners, and value...
The Platform Case		Jun 1919263%/37%8406
Full post content
I've had several conversations this week with VC and PE partners, and value creation kept coming up. My view that GTM and sales acceleration are the primary active return levers received mixed reactions: in PE, accepted reality, but in VC, still slightly heretical. The PE logic is clean: you buy the company, the clock starts, and over a 3-4 year hold, you have a finite set of levers you can actually pull. You're not re-architecting the product. You're not waiting for the market to mature. What you can move in the time you have is the commercial engine: pricing, motion, win rates, revenue per rep. Operating partners like me live there because that's where the return is. VC reflexes run the other way: product and market first, find fit, and the rest follows. GTM gets treated as downstream of PMF, a VP Sales you bring in eighteen months later. Fine at pre-see, of course, but it is expensive at (scaling up) seed and Series A, when growth stalls and the board reaches for the only two explanations the reflex allows: the product needs more, or we need more reps. Neither is right, in my experience. Go through the last twenty closed-lost deals, and what you find is almost always architecture, or the lack of it. The motion that closed the first ten customers ran on the founder being in the room, and, of course, nobody wrote it down. So every rep now sells it differently, and the pipeline reflects optimism rather than evidence. The product was rarely the problem. The motion was never built. This is where the comparison breaks down, and PE fixes it by taking control and embedding operators, while VC can't (a minority stake, at most a board seat, a founder who has to want the help, etc.). Same lever, pulled differently. For any serious VC, that's the case for a platform function and for GTM operating partners inside it. Not a VP Sales parachuted into one company, but a commercial capability the fund can point to across the whole portfolio, that diagnoses the motion before the board defaults to "hire more reps." I resisted this conclusion for a long time, but PE worked this out a decade ago. VC is where PE was before operating partners became table stakes. The commercial engine is the lever and the only question is whether you've built the function to pull it. View on LinkedIn →

I spent most of my operating years inside one company at a time, and from that seat you only ever see one experiment running. An operator inside a single company sees one motion and one hiring cycle play out, while an operator sitting across a portfolio sees the same failure forming in three companies before any of the three founders has felt it. That is the asset a single VP of Sales can never be. I have learned that the failures repeat because they are structural, not local. And whether you are on the investor side or inside the company, we have all seen this sequence run. For example, a founder-to-team transition in which the first senior sales hire underperforms, and the founder blames the hire. Or hiring a VP of Sales at roughly £150K fully loaded, allowing 9 months to deliver results, underperformance, then unwinding the role. Looks like a misshire, but in reality, it is structural. Each was attempting to scale sales by adding senior talent to a go-to-market motion that had never been clearly defined or proven. Headcount went up, but so did burn, and progress often stalled. That is where the fund maths hurts. A mis-timed senior GTM hire is not a single line in one company's P&L because if you multiply that misread across the portfolio, it stops being an operating cost and starts dragging DPI. Capital going into headcount before the motion exists easily turns into five-year holds carrying the interest on that mistake. And I will confess I did not see this from the outside. The first time I sat in this pattern I was inside one of the companies, and I read it as our problem and our bad hire. It took a second and a third company, from a different seat, to realise I had been misreading a structural pattern as a local accident. Funding a VP of Sales in each company buys you competent execution and a single data point. Funding the seat that watches them all buys pattern recognition early enough to change the outcome. One is a cost line. The other is underwriting. View on LinkedIn →

I have not sat in on a VC fund review this year where operational value creation wasn't named as the lever, but naming it and building to pull it are two different things. Today, with more expensive debt and a multiple that has stopped climbing, the same return requires closer to 10-12% earnings growth per year. When I got into the industry about 9 years ago, operational value creation was just a line in the deck, but the deal math turned it into the job. And there is the operator who sits in the room when the deal is underwritten, owns a number, and carries it to exit. The first is a cost line. The second is a returns lever, and a returns lever has to be senior, inside the investment decision, and paid like the partners it sits next to. And, like it wasn't enough, the bar moved again in the past 18 months or so: AI stopped being a theme and became operating infrastructure: inside portfolio companies, in go-to-market, in code, in how deals get researched. +"Our ICP is defined." The head of sales at a portfolio company told me this...
Value Creation		Jul 2313980%/20%6500
Full post content
"Our ICP is defined." The head of sales at a portfolio company (Series A, $2.9m revenue) told me this recently with complete confidence, but what they'd actually defined was two job titles joined by an "or": VP of Engineering or Head of Platform. That's a persona, and starting with the persona is the right instinct, because they carry the job-to-be-done and feel the pain you are leaning into. But a persona on its own is not a target. The same title is a different buyer depending on the company behind it. For example, a Head of Engineering at Facebook and a Head of Engineering at a 40-person startup share almost nothing: different budget, authority, pain, reason to move. Same job, opposite drivers. The persona tells you who feels the pain, but the ICP tells you which companies make that pain funded, urgent and repeatable. Skip that second half, and you hit one of two walls: --> Happy ears: you get the meeting, the nod, the "this really matters", and then nothing moves. No driver behind the pain, no budget, no mandate. You read enthusiasm as pipeline, and it dies in "no decision." --> Or silence: you can't get the meeting at all, because your positioning speaks to a driver this persona doesn't carry here. Low replies, dead outbound, "not a priority." Both look exactly like a product problem, and many decide they haven't found product-market fit and go back to build more, or pivot away. Look, often the pain is real, the persona is right, and the account is wrong. That is not a PMF gap to me, but an ICP and positioning gap that looks identical from the inside. Same product, put in front of the company with the driver, moves. So before you rebuild the product, check that you are not selling the right pain to the wrong company. Here's the cheat sheet I used with any early-stage portfolio company I work with. Hope it helps someone. View on LinkedIn →
